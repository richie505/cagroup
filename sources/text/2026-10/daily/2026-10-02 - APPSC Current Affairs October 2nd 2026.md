Rayalaseema Global Horticulture Hub

### Relevance: APPSC: Andhra Pradesh Economy, Agriculture, Rayalaseema Development and Current Affairs
Prelims: Global Horticulture Hub | Rayalaseema Rising | Horticulture Clusters | Drip Irrigation | Food Processing | Export Infrastructure | Post-harvest Losses
Mains: Agricultural Diversification | Value-chain Development | Farmer Income | Public–Private Investment | Regional Development | Export-oriented Agriculture

## Why in News?
The Andhra Pradesh government is launching the ₹1 lakh crore Global Horticulture Hub at Madanapalle to transform Rayalaseema into a major fruit-production, processing and export region.Union Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman , Chief Minister N. Chandrababu Naidu and Deputy Chief Minister Pawan Kalyan are associated with the launch.

## What is the Horticulture Hub?
The project seeks to develop an integrated horticulture ecosystem covering:
- irrigation and drip-irrigation systems;
- expansion of fruit cultivation;
- rural roads and logistics;
- food-processing facilities;
- storage and supply chains;
- and export infrastructure.
The government aims to give Andhra Pradesh a “fruit bowl” identity , alongside its traditional image as a “rice bowl”.

## What is its Geographical Coverage?
The project will cover:
- 10 districts
- 303 mandals
- 5,869 villages
- 201 horticulture clusters
- approximately 9.6 lakh farmers

## What are its Major Targets?
Cultivation
- Existing horticulture area: 8.41 lakh hectares
- Proposed area: 14.41 lakh hectares
- Additional area: 6 lakh hectares
Production
- Existing production: 222 lakh metric tonnes
- Targeted production: 435 lakh metric tonnes
Irrigation
- Create irrigation potential for approximately 9 lakh acres .
- Stabilise irrigation facilities over another 4.3 lakh acres .

## What is the Investment Plan?
The overall proposed investment is approximately ₹1 lakh crore .
Government Investment: ₹40,352 Crore
- Irrigation: ₹20,884 crore
- Horticulture development: ₹14,906 crore
- Rural roads: ₹4,562 crore
Private Investment: Around ₹60,000 Crore
Private investment will mainly be encouraged in:
- food-processing units;
- value-addition facilities;
- logistics;
- supply-chain development;
- and export infrastructure.
The detailed public-investment components together amount to the proposed government outlay of ₹40,352 crore.

## What Infrastructure Will be Developed?
The project proposes:
- irrigation networks;
- drip-irrigation facilities;
- rural roads;
- farm-to-market connectivity;
- logistics and transport systems;
- food-processing units;
- storage and packaging facilities;
- and export-support infrastructure.
These facilities are intended to connect production areas with processing centres and domestic and international markets.

## Which Crops Will be Promoted?
Major horticultural crops proposed for promotion include:
Mango | Banana | Tomato | Pomegranate | Dragon Fruit | Avocado | Berries
Other high-value fruits and vegetables may also be developed according to regional suitability and market demand.

## What are the Expected Outcomes?
- Higher production: Nearly double horticultural output.
- Crop diversification: Reduce excessive dependence on conventional field crops.
- Farmer income: Improve returns through processing, exports and better market access.
- Reduced losses: Limit losses between harvesting and final sale.
- Value addition: Encourage grading, packaging and food processing.
- Export growth: Develop Rayalaseema as an export-oriented horticulture region.
- Regional development: Create economic opportunities in a drought-prone region.
- Private participation: Mobilise capital for processing, logistics and exports.

## Conclusion
The Global Horticulture Hub seeks to transform Rayalaseema through irrigation expansion, high-value cultivation, food processing and export connectivity. Its success will depend on coordinated implementation, efficient water use and strong market linkages that convert higher production into better farmer incomes.

Bharat Maritime Insurance Pool: A Domestic Safety Net for Maritime Trade

### Relevance: UPSC GS Paper III: Shipping, ports, insurance, infrastructure and economic security
Prelims: BMIP | GIC Re | P&I Insurance | Hull and Machinery | Cargo War Cover | Sovereign Guarantee | Gross Tonnage | Exclusive Economic Zone
Mains: Maritime Sovereignty | Trade Resilience | Energy Security | Domestic Risk Capacity | Financial Self-reliance | Blue Economy | Indian Ocean Region

## Why in News?
India operationalised the Bharat Maritime Insurance Pool in May 2026 to create an indigenous insurance mechanism for ships, cargo and maritime liabilities.By 7 September 2026 , the Pool had issued 3,000 Cargo War policies, 92 Hull War-risk policies and three Protection and Indemnity policies . Its early performance shows India’s attempt to move from dependence on foreign insurers towards domestic maritime risk management.

## Why is the Maritime Sector Important to India?
- Trade Dependence: Around 95% of India’s trade by value and 70% by volume is transported through maritime routes.
- Energy Security: A major portion of India’s crude-oil and energy imports moves by sea, making uninterrupted shipping essential for economic stability.
- Port Network: India operates 12 major ports and 217 non-major ports .
- Cargo Movement: Indian ports handled approximately 1,668 million metric tonnes of cargo during 2025–26 .
- Coastline: India’s coastline extends for nearly 11,098 kilometres , providing access to major international shipping routes.
- Marine Resources: India’s 2.4-million-square-kilometre Exclusive Economic Zone provides sovereign rights over fisheries and other marine resources.
- Inland Transport: More than 14,500 kilometres of rivers, canals and backwaters can support cost-effective freight transport.
- Livelihoods: Maritime industries sustain more than 30 million people , including fishers, seafarers, port workers and shipbuilders.
- Growing Fleet: By mid-2026, India’s flagged fleet included 1,609 ships with 14.33 million gross tonnage , marking nearly 36% tonnage growth since 2015 .
- Premium Outflow: Dependence on overseas insurers causes an annual outflow of approximately USD 45–60 million in P&I premiums , besides other marine-insurance payments.

## What is the Bharat Maritime Insurance Pool?
BMIP is India’s first collective domestic platform for sharing and insuring large maritime risks.
- Approval: 18 April 2026
- Formal launch: 12 May 2026
- Insurance capacity: ₹13,906.50 crore, equivalent to about USD 1.5 billion
- Sovereign guarantee: ₹12,980 crore, equivalent to about USD 1.4 billion
- Initial duration: 10 years
- Maximum duration after extension: 15 years
- Administrator: General Insurance Corporation of India

### Objectives
BMIP seeks to:
- build domestic marine-insurance capacity;
- reduce premium payments to foreign markets;
- provide affordable insurance;
- maintain coverage during international disruptions;
- and develop Indian expertise in underwriting and claims settlement.

### Eligibility
BMIP coverage is available for:
- Indian-flagged ships;
- vessels owned by Indian entities;
- vessels managed or controlled by Indian entities;
- and cargo vessels beginning their journey in India or travelling to India.

## How Does the Pool Function?
- Domestic Policy Issuance: Insurance policies are issued by participating Indian insurers.
- Shared Underwriting: Pool members combine their capacity to cover maritime risks that may be too large for an individual insurer.
- Collective Reinsurance: Accepted risks are reinsured among the members according to their committed share.
- Routine Claims: Claims up to USD 100 million are settled from accumulated reserves and reinsurance recoveries.
- Catastrophic Claims: The sovereign guarantee becomes available for larger claims only after the Pool’s reserves have been fully used.

## Governance Structure
- GIC Re: Manages daily operations and performance reporting.
- Governing Body: Provides supervision and ensures regulatory compliance.
- Underwriting Committee: Examines risks and promotes consistent and financially sustainable pricing.

## What Protection Does BMIP Offer?

### Hull and Machinery Insurance
It covers damage to:
- the ship’s main structure;
- propulsion machinery;
- and installed equipment.
This protects the capital value of vessels.

### Cargo War Insurance
It protects goods against loss or damage while moving through conflict-affected or high-risk maritime routes.

### Protection and Indemnity Insurance
P&I coverage protects shipowners against third-party liabilities such as:
- marine pollution;
- oil-spill clean-up;
- wreck removal;
- crew injuries;
- collision liabilities;
- and cargo damage.

### War-perils Insurance
It protects vessels and cargo against:
- armed conflict;
- piracy;
- terrorism;
- hostile seizure;
- and related risks in sensitive maritime corridors.

## Why was BMIP Necessary?
- Foreign Insurance Concentration: Indian shipowners have depended heavily on the 13 clubs belonging to the International Group of P&I Clubs .
- Global Market Dominance: These clubs insure nearly 90% of the world’s large ships , giving overseas institutions substantial influence over insurance availability and pricing.
- Risk of Withdrawal: Foreign insurers may suspend or restrict coverage when ships enter sanctioned, conflict-affected or politically sensitive regions.
- Premium Volatility: War and geopolitical instability can cause sudden increases in maritime-insurance premiums.
- Critical Trade Routes: Disruptions in the Red Sea and Strait of Hormuz affect routes important for Indian trade and energy imports.
- Industrial Exposure: A shortage of insurance may interrupt the movement of raw materials and essential goods required by Indian industries.
- Domestic Expertise Gap: India previously had limited institutional capacity in specialised marine underwriting and complex claims management.
- Foreign-exchange Outflow: Domestic insurance allows a larger share of premiums to remain within the country.
- Strategic Autonomy: Sovereign-backed coverage ensures that India’s maritime operations are not entirely dependent on decisions taken in foreign insurance centres.

## What has BMIP Achieved?
- Rapid Policy Issuance: By 7 September 2026, the Pool had issued 3,000 Cargo War, 92 Hull War-risk and three P&I policies.
- Lower Premiums: War-risk premiums declined by approximately 35–40% from their peak during the West Asia conflict .
- Continuous Protection: Indian maritime stakeholders continued to receive War-risk coverage despite unstable international conditions.
- First Policy: On 12 May 2026, New India Assurance issued a Hull and Machinery War-risk policy to Hoger Offshore and Marine Private Limited for a vessel operating through conflict zones.
- First P&I Policy: On 30 July 2026, the first P&I policy under BMIP was issued to the Shipping Corporation of India Limited by New India Assurance.
- Protection of Industrial Imports: Vedanta Sterlite Copper Limited received a Marine Cargo War Policy for its imports.
- Coverage Beyond Shipowners: A policy issued to Balrampur Chini Mills Limited showed that manufacturers, traders and other cargo-dependent businesses can also use the Pool.
- Institutional Development: Systems for underwriting, claims settlement, reporting and regulatory compliance have been established.
- Maritime Sovereignty: India has gained greater control over insurance for its vessels, cargo and sea-based supply chains.

## What Should be the Future Focus?
- Specialised Skills: Develop marine underwriters, actuaries, surveyors, claims experts and maritime-law professionals.
- Accurate Pricing: Use vessel, route and cargo data to determine premiums according to the level of risk.
- Adequate Reserves: Build sufficient financial reserves to meet large claims without frequently relying on sovereign support.
- Reliable Claims Settlement: Ensure transparent and timely processing of claims to build market confidence.
- Reinsurance Partnerships: Maintain strong reinsurance arrangements for risks exceeding domestic capacity.
- Legal Strengthening: Improve rules relating to maritime liability, dispute settlement and insurance enforcement.
- Wider Participation: Encourage more insurers, shipping companies, exporters and importers to join the domestic system.
- Regional Expansion: Develop BMIP gradually into a maritime-insurance platform serving the wider Indian Ocean Region.
- Alignment with Maritime Growth: Expand insurance capacity as India’s fleet, ports, shipbuilding industry and cargo volumes increase.

## Conclusion
The Bharat Maritime Insurance Pool provides India with an indigenous mechanism to protect ships, cargo and maritime supply chains. It strengthens financial self-reliance by reducing exposure to foreign insurance decisions and retaining risk-management expertise within the country. Prudent underwriting, strong reserves and credible claims settlement can transform BMIP into a durable pillar of India’s maritime infrastructure.

Gandhi Jayanti 2026: Mahatma Gandhi’s Role, Philosophy and Contemporary Relevance

### Relevance: UPSC GS Paper I: Modern Indian History and Indian National Movement
Prelims: Gandhi Jayanti | 2 October 1869 | Porbandar | Satyagraha | Ahimsa | Swaraj | Sarvodaya | Trusteeship | Swadeshi | Champaran Satyagraha | Kheda Satyagraha | Ahmedabad Mill Strike | Non-Cooperation Movement | Dandi March | Gandhi-Irwin Pact | Second Round Table Conference | Quit India Movement | Belgaum Session | Hind Swaraj | International Day of Non-Violence | Swachh Bharat Mission
Mains: Non-violent resistance | Mass mobilisation | Ethical politics | Constructive programme | Economic self-reliance | Social justice | Communal harmony | Rural development | Responsible consumption | Participatory governance | Sustainable development

## Why in News?
- Gandhi Jayanti was observed on 2 October 2026 , marking the 157th birth anniversary of Mahatma Gandhi .
- The day is also observed by the United Nations as the International Day of Non-Violence , recognising Gandhi’s enduring contribution to peace and non-violent resistance.

## About Mahatma Gandhi?
- Birth: Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi was born on 2 October 1869 at Porbandar in the Kathiawar region of present-day Gujarat.
- Family: He was the youngest child of Karamchand Gandhi , who served as Diwan in several princely States, and Putlibai , whose religious outlook influenced his ideas of truth and self-discipline.
- Marriage: He married Kasturba Gandhi in 1883 . They had four sons—Harilal, Manilal, Ramdas and Devdas.
- Education: After studying in Porbandar and Rajkot, Gandhi travelled to England in 1888 to study law and was called to the Bar in 1891 .
- South African Phase: He went to South Africa in 1893 , where his experience of racial discrimination, including his removal from a first-class railway compartment at Pietermaritzburg , shaped his struggle against injustice.
- Development of Satyagraha: During more than two decades in South Africa, he organised the Indian community and developed Satyagraha as resistance based on truth and non-violence.
- Return to India: Gandhi returned to India in 1915 and gradually became a central leader of the national movement.
- Death: He was assassinated by Nathuram Godse on 30 January 1948 in New Delhi.

## How did Gandhi Transform the National Movement?
- Mass Mobilisation: Gandhi expanded the freedom struggle beyond educated elites by involving peasants, workers, women, students and traders.
- Non-violent Politics: He made Satyagraha, non-cooperation and civil disobedience the principal methods of challenging colonial rule.
- Economic Nationalism: He promoted Swadeshi, khadi and spinning as instruments of self-reliance and resistance to colonial economic exploitation.
- Social Reform: Gandhi connected political freedom with the removal of untouchability, communal harmony, sanitation and rural reconstruction.
- Ethical Discipline: He maintained that political movements must remain non-violent and was prepared to suspend them when violence occurred.
- Constructive Programme: Village industries, cleanliness, basic education and community participation became integral to his vision of Swaraj.

## What were Gandhi’s Major Movements?

### Early Satyagrahas in India
- Champaran Satyagraha, 1917: Gandhi supported peasants subjected to exploitation under the indigo cultivation system.
- Ahmedabad Mill Strike, 1918: He intervened in a dispute between textile workers and mill owners.
- Kheda Satyagraha, 1918: He supported peasants seeking land-revenue relief after crop failure.
These movements established Gandhi’s ability to connect local economic grievances with the broader anti-colonial struggle.

### Non-Cooperation Movement, 1920–22
- The movement emerged against the background of the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and the Khilafat issue .
- Gandhi argued that colonial rule depended upon Indian cooperation and could be weakened by its peaceful withdrawal.
- People were encouraged to boycott colonial institutions and promote Swadeshi and khadi .
- Gandhi withdrew the movement after the Chauri Chaura incident of February 1922 , in which protesters killed policemen.
- Its withdrawal demonstrated Gandhi’s insistence that mass politics must remain disciplined and non-violent.

### Civil Disobedience Movement and Dandi March
- The movement began in 1930 through the deliberate, peaceful violation of unjust colonial laws.
- Gandhi chose the salt tax because salt was an essential commodity affecting all social groups.
- He began the Dandi March from Sabarmati Ashram on 12 March 1930 and walked approximately 240 miles before breaking the salt law at Dandi.
- The movement attracted peasants, women, workers, students and traders. Women participated prominently in picketing and protest activities.
- It resulted in widespread arrests but compelled the colonial government to negotiate, leading to the Gandhi-Irwin Pact of 1931 .

### Second Round Table Conference
- The Gandhi-Irwin Pact enabled Gandhi to attend the Second Round Table Conference in London in 1931 as the sole representative of the Indian National Congress.
- He demanded responsible government and India’s right to determine its constitutional future.
- Gandhi opposed constitutional arrangements based on separate electorates, believing they could deepen social divisions.
- Disagreements over minority representation prevented a settlement. Gandhi returned without securing Congress demands, and the Civil Disobedience Movement resumed in 1932 .

### Quit India Movement, 1942
- The movement was launched in August 1942 after the failure of the Cripps Mission .
- Gandhi gave the historic call of “Do or Die” and demanded an immediate end to British rule.
- Gandhi and other leaders were arrested soon after its launch, but resistance spread across the country.
- It became the final major nationwide mass movement led by Gandhi and demonstrated the depth of popular opposition to British rule.

### Belgaum Congress Session
- Gandhi presided over the Indian National Congress session at Belgaum in 1924 .
- It was the only Congress session over which he presided as President.
- The session reflected his emphasis on khadi, communal harmony, removal of untouchability and constructive work.

## What are the Core Ideas of Gandhian Philosophy?
- Satyagraha: Adherence to truth and resistance to injustice through non-violent methods. For Gandhi, it was a moral principle rather than merely a political strategy.
- Ahimsa: Resistance to injustice without hatred or physical violence. It required courage, discipline and respect for human dignity.
- Swaraj: It meant both political self-rule and individual self-discipline, responsibility and moral autonomy.
- Sarvodaya: It referred to the welfare of all, particularly the weakest and most disadvantaged sections.
- Trusteeship: Wealth-holders should treat their resources as a social trust and use them for the wider welfare of society.
- Swadeshi: It promoted indigenous production, local economic activity and self-reliance. Khadi and the charkha became its major symbols.

## What were Gandhi’s Important Works?
- Hind Swaraj (1909): Presented Gandhi’s critique of modern civilisation and his understanding of Swaraj.
- The Story of My Experiments with Truth: Gandhi’s autobiography, explaining the development of his ethical, spiritual and political ideas.

## What is Gandhi’s International Legacy?
- The United Nations General Assembly adopted a resolution in 2007 declaring 2 October the International Day of Non-Violence .
- Its objective is to promote non-violence, peace, tolerance and mutual understanding.
- Gandhi’s methods subsequently influenced civil-rights and political-freedom movements across the world.

## How is Gandhi Jayanti Observed in India?
- Gandhi Jayanti is one of India’s three national holidays , along with Republic Day and Independence Day .
- The Government of India’s 2026 calendar lists 2 October as a gazetted holiday.
- National commemorations and tributes are organised at Rajghat in New Delhi , where Gandhi’s memorial is located.
- Restrictions on alcohol sales are imposed in several parts of India, although dry-day rules are determined by the excise laws of individual States and Union Territories.

## How is Gandhi Jayanti Connected with the Swachh Bharat Mission?
- The Swachh Bharat Mission was launched on 2 October 2014 , linking sanitation and cleanliness with Gandhi’s emphasis on personal and community hygiene.
- Swachh Bharat Mission–Grameen focuses on rural sanitation and behavioural change.
- Its second phase emphasises sustaining Open Defecation Free status and improving solid and liquid waste management .
- The mission gives contemporary expression to Gandhi’s principles of cleanliness, self-reliance and community participation.

## What is the Contemporary Relevance of Gandhian Thought?
- Peaceful Conflict Resolution: Ahimsa and Satyagraha offer methods of resolving disputes through dialogue and resistance without violence.
- Ethical Governance: Gandhi’s emphasis on self-discipline, accountability and public service can strengthen integrity in administration.
- Social Justice: Sarvodaya provides an ethical basis for policies centred on vulnerable and disadvantaged communities.
- Sustainable Development: Moderation and responsible consumption can help address environmental degradation and unsustainable lifestyles.
- Local Self-Reliance: Swadeshi, village industries and community participation remain relevant for decentralised and inclusive development.
- Communal Harmony: Gandhi’s commitment to social unity remains important in addressing polarisation and identity-based conflict.
- Citizen Participation: His constructive programme demonstrates that political freedom must be supported by active civic responsibility.

## Conclusion
Mahatma Gandhi transformed India’s freedom struggle by combining mass participation with truth, non-violence and ethical discipline. His vision went beyond political independence to include social justice, economic self-reliance, communal harmony and responsible citizenship. Gandhian principles remain relevant to peaceful conflict resolution, sustainable development and good governance. Their contemporary application can contribute to an inclusive and ethically grounded Viksit Bharat by 2047 .
