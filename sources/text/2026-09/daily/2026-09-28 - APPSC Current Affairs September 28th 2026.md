Andhra Pradesh Social Audit Body Wins SKOCH Gold Award

## Why in News?
The Society for Social Audit Accountability and Transparency of Andhra Pradesh (SSAAT) has received the SKOCH Gold Award for strengthening public participation, transparency and accountability in government programmes.It became the first State Social Audit Organisation in India to receive this award.Andhra Pradesh Social Audit Director G. Srikanth received the award at the 109th SKOCH Summit , held at the India Habitat Centre in New Delhi.

## About SSAAT
SSAAT is Andhra Pradesh’s social audit organisation. It works to improve accountability in the implementation of rural development and welfare programmes.
Its approach places citizens at the centre of the audit process by allowing them to:
- Participate in field-level audits.
- Examine the implementation of government programmes.
- Raise concerns before public authorities.
- Access social audit findings.
- Participate in Gram Sabhas and public hearings.
The State’s social audit initiatives have been undertaken under the guidance of the Minister for Panchayat Raj and Rural Development.

## What is a Social Audit?
A social audit is a public examination of how a government programme is implemented at the ground level.
It allows citizens and beneficiaries to check whether:
- Government benefits have reached eligible people.
- Public funds have been properly used.
- Programme records match actual work completed.
- Officials and implementing agencies have followed prescribed procedures.
- Problems and irregularities have been addressed.
Unlike a financial audit, which mainly examines accounts and expenditure, a social audit focuses on people’s participation and the actual delivery of public services.

## Initiatives Taken by SSAAT

### Public Participation in Field Audits
- SSAAT has involved large numbers of citizens in field-level social audits.
- This enables local people to compare official records with actual conditions and programme implementation.

### Presentation of Findings
Audit findings are presented before:
- Gram Sabhas.
- Public hearings.
- Citizens.
- Beneficiaries.
- Officials concerned with programme implementation.
This gives affected people an opportunity to raise questions and seek corrective action.

### Livestreaming of Proceedings
- Public proceedings are livestreamed to improve openness and allow wider public observation of the social audit process.

### Digitisation of Reports
- Social audit reports are being digitised and made available for wider public access.
- Digitisation helps citizens, officials and other stakeholders examine findings without depending only on physical records.

## Role of Gram Sabhas and Public Hearings

### Gram Sabha
- The Gram Sabha provides a local platform where residents can examine audit findings and discuss problems in the implementation of rural development and welfare programmes.

### Public Hearing
- A public hearing allows citizens and beneficiaries to present grievances, question irregularities and seek responses from the authorities concerned.
- Together, Gram Sabhas and public hearings make the audit process more participatory and publicly accountable.

## SKOCH Governance Assessment

Its assessment process includes:
- Primary research.

- Field validation.
- Identification of systemic problems.

- Evidence-based recommendations.
SKOCH has also developed indices covering:

Its knowledge resources include policy briefs, research reports, books, journals, workshops, conferences, exhibitions and documentation of governance practices.

## Conclusion
The SKOCH Gold Award recognises Andhra Pradesh’s efforts to make social audits more participatory, transparent and accessible. The long-term value of the mechanism will depend on regular citizen participation, reliable public records and timely action on audit findings.

## Why in News?
World Tourism Day 2026 was observed on 27 September under the theme “Digital Agenda and Artificial Intelligence to Redesign Tourism.” The global observance was held in El Salvador . The theme highlighted the role of digital innovation, data and Artificial Intelligence in transforming tourism planning, destination management and visitor experiences.

## About World Tourism Day
- World Tourism Day is an initiative of the United Nations World Tourism Organisation (UNWTO) . It is observed annually on 27 September to commemorate the adoption of the UNWTO statutes in 1970. It was first celebrated in 1980.
- The day highlights tourism’s contribution to socio-economic development and the Sustainable Development Goals .

### Theme for 2026
- The theme “Digital Agenda and Artificial Intelligence to Redesign Tourism” focuses on using technology and AI to improve decision-making, strengthen skills, personalise travel, manage destinations and make tourism more inclusive and sustainable.

## World Tourism Day Celebrations in India

### Central Celebration
- The central celebration was held at Bharat Mandapam in New Delhi .
- The Ministry of Tourism announced the National Digital Tourism Stack and Dekho Apna Desh 2.0 .
- The Ministry also proposed partnerships with industry organisations to improve destination visibility, digital outreach, experiential tourism, sustainable tourism and community participation under the Incredible India framework.

### Telangana
- Telangana organised cultural programmes, workshops, tourism film screenings, concerts and artisanal stalls displaying food, handlooms and handicrafts.
- The Telangana State Tourism Awards recognised travel agents, tour operators, classified hotels, restaurants, independent hotels and other participants in the tourism industry.

### Delhi
- The Delhi Government organised “RANG • RAS • ROOH” at Baansera, the bamboo park at Sarai Kale Khan on the banks of the Yamuna, on 26–27 September.
- The programme combined art, culture, food, entertainment and tourism experiences. It also included a Qawwali performance by the Nizami Brothers.

## Status of Global Tourism
In 2024, global travel and tourism contributed US$10.9 trillion , accounting for around 10% of global GDP . The sector supported approximately 357 million jobs and generated US$1.9 trillion in international visitor spending.

### Status of Tourism in India
- Tourism connects people with India’s culture, heritage, natural landscapes and communities. It also generates employment, supports local businesses and contributes to foreign exchange earnings.

### Tourism Satellite Account
- The Tourism Satellite Account (TSA) is the official accounting framework used to measure tourism’s direct and indirect economic contribution.
- Tourism contributes 5.22% to India’s GDP . Employment in the sector increased from 6.94 crore in 2019-20 to 8.46 crore in 2023-24 .

### Growth in Tourist Visits
- Domestic Tourist Visits increased from 67.7 crore in 2021 to 428.55 crore in 2025 . Foreign Tourist Visits increased from 0.1 crore to 2.53 crore during the same period.
- Foreign exchange earnings from tourism rose from ₹63,978 crore in 2021 to ₹2,76,831 crore in 2025 .

## Digital Transformation of Tourism
- Tourism is moving from physical maps, guidebooks and manual bookings towards smartphones, online platforms and AI-enabled services.
- Digital technology now helps travellers discover destinations, plan journeys, book transport and accommodation, obtain real-time weather information, explore cities, purchase tickets and receive emergency assistance.
- AI can analyse a traveller’s interests, budget and preferences to provide personalised travel plans. It can also improve destination management, tourism promotion and decision-making.

## Major Digital Tourism Initiatives

### National Digital Tourism Stack
- The National Digital Tourism Stack is an open digital public infrastructure initiative. It seeks to make tourism offerings more discoverable, accessible and trustworthy.
- It can connect information about destinations, transport, accommodation, service providers and visitor facilities through a common digital ecosystem.

### Dekho Apna Desh 2.0
- Dekho Apna Desh 2.0 focuses on tourism-ready but lesser-known destinations. It seeks to promote domestic tourism, reduce pressure on overcrowded destinations, distribute tourist footfall more evenly and generate local livelihoods.

### e-Visa Facility
- India introduced Electronic Travel Authorisation in 2014 for nationals of 43 countries. The e-Visa facility is now available to nationals of 172 countries .
- Entry is permitted through 88 international ports—37 airports, 38 seaports and 13 land ports . India issues approximately 78% of its visas electronically , while nearly 95% of e-Visa applications are processed within 72 hours .

## Incredible India Digital Platform
- The revamped Incredible India Digital Platform presents virtual experiences related to culture, heritage, adventure, gastronomy, wellness, arts and crafts, and rural tourism.
- Its AI-powered services provide personalised information, real-time weather updates, city-exploration support and essential travel services.
- The platform connects travellers with Online Travel Agents for booking flights, hotels, cabs and buses. It also supports ticket booking for Archaeological Survey of India monuments.
- Its integration with NIDHI provides information about registered tour operators and travel agents.

### NIDHI+
- NIDHI+ , or the National Integrated Database of Hospitality Industry , is a unified digital platform for the registration, recognition and classification of tourism service providers.
- It provides end-to-end online processing, integrated payments and transparent approvals while improving ease of doing business.

### Google India Partnership
- In June 2026, the Ministry of Tourism signed an MoU with Google India to promote Indian tourism through digital technologies, AI, data-driven insights and capacity building.
- The partnership uses information on global travel trends, traveller behaviour and digital engagement. It also includes training for Ministry officials in digital marketing, campaign optimisation, content creation and emerging technologies.

### Multilingual Tourist Helpline
- The Ministry operates a 24×7 multilingual Tourist Helpline through the toll-free number 1800111363 and short code 1363 .
- It provides information in 12 international languages, including Arabic, French, German, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Portuguese, Russian and Spanish . It also assists tourists during emergencies and alerts the authorities when required.

### Tourism Start-ups and Innovation
- As of 17 April 2023, India’s travel and tourism sector had 1,497 DPIIT-recognised start-ups across 262 districts , generating employment for nearly 13,919 people . About 58% were located in Tier II and Tier III cities.
- These start-ups operate in travel technology, online booking, homestays, rural tourism, experiential tourism, smart mobility and AI-enabled travel planning.
- Highway Delite has created a digitally connected highway-amenities network; VilloTale promotes rural tourism through homestays and community-based experiences; and udChalo provides technology-enabled travel services to more than 2.8 million defence personnel, veterans and their dependants through digital platforms and over 70 booking offices.

## India’s Diverse Tourism Landscape

### Spiritual and Pilgrimage Tourism
- India has important pilgrimage destinations associated with Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism. Major destinations include Ayodhya, Kashi, Bodh Gaya, Amritsar, Tirupati and Puri .
- PRASHAD and Swadesh Darshan support infrastructure and visitor facilities at religious destinations.

### Buddhist Tourism
- Bodh Gaya in Bihar and Sarnath and Kushinagar in Uttar Pradesh are important sites associated with the life of the Buddha.
- These destinations reflect India’s historical and civilisational connections with Buddhist communities across Asia.

### Heritage and Cultural Tourism
- India’s cultural tourism resources include forts, palaces, temples, archaeological sites, museums, historic cities, classical music, dance, festivals, handicrafts and regional cuisines.

### Wellness, Yoga and Medical Tourism
- India’s traditions of Yoga, Ayurveda and other traditional systems of medicine give the country a distinct position in wellness tourism. These traditions are supported by modern healthcare facilities and medical services.

### Eco-tourism and Wildlife Tourism
- National parks, tiger reserves, wetlands, mangroves, forests and Himalayan landscapes support wildlife safaris, birdwatching, trekking and nature trails.
- Responsible tourism can promote conservation while creating livelihoods for communities living around these destinations.

### Rural, Homestay and Community-based Tourism
- Homestays, village experiences, local cuisine, handicrafts, agricultural activities and community-led experiences provide visitors with closer exposure to rural life.
- They can generate additional income for rural households, artisans, local enterprises and tribal communities.

### Coastal, Cruise and Experiential Tourism
- Sea cruise passenger traffic increased from 1.08 lakh in 2014-15 to 4.62 lakh in 2025-26 , recording 328% growth .
- International cruise terminals at Visakhapatnam, Mumbai and Chennai have been upgraded and operationalised. Puducherry has been added to the East Coast Cruise Circuit.
- A QR-based immigration system has reduced clearance time to around 30 seconds per passenger . Digitised customs procedures and e-Group Visit Visas also support smoother entry for international cruise groups.

### Lighthouse Tourism
- Lighthouses traditionally function as Marine Aids to Navigation .
- Under Section 23 of the Marine Aids to Navigation Act, 2021, the government is promoting lighthouses for educational, cultural and tourism purposes.
- Lighthouse museums at Chennai, Mahabalipuram, Alappuzha, Kannur, Muttom, Dwarka and Gopnath present India’s maritime history and the evolution of lighthouse technology.

### MICE Tourism
- MICE tourism refers to Meetings, Incentives, Conferences and Exhibitions .
- India is strengthening its business-events ecosystem through Bharat Mandapam and Yashobhoomi in Delhi and the Jio World Centre in Mumbai.
- The government has identified the potential to develop at least 10 Indian cities , particularly in South India, as global MICE destinations.
- The global MICE industry is projected to grow from US$870 billion to more than US$1.4 trillion by 2030 . India currently holds approximately 5% of the global market .

## International Tourism Cooperation

### BRICS and the Jaipur Declaration
- During India’s BRICS Chairship in 2026, the BRICS Tourism Ministers’ Meeting was held in Jaipur and adopted the Jaipur Declaration .
- Its four priorities are AI and tourism, sustainable and responsible tourism, tourism skilling and capacity building, and tourism exchanges with seamless travel facilitation .
- It also supports cooperation in tourism planning, destination management, visitor services and multilingual communication.

### G20 and Tourism Promotion
- During India’s G20 Presidency, more than 200 meetings were held across 60 cities .
- These meetings presented India’s heritage, regional cuisines and local crafts to an international audience. They also promoted destinations beyond metropolitan cities and demonstrated India’s capacity to organise major global events.

### Infrastructure-led Destination Development
- India is moving from developing isolated attractions towards creating integrated destination ecosystems. Such an ecosystem combines tourism attractions, transport connectivity, accommodation, visitor facilities, safety, cleanliness, digital services and community participation.

### Regional Air Connectivity
- A total of 687 UDAN routes connecting 95 airports have been operationalised.The Modified UDAN scheme for 2026-27 to 2035-36 has an approved outlay of ₹28,840 crore and proposes the development of 100 airports and 200 modern helipads .These measures seek to improve regional and last-mile access to tourist destinations, particularly in underserved regions.

## Major Government Initiatives

### Swadesh Darshan
- Swadesh Darshan was launched in 2014-15 to develop thematic tourism circuits.Under the scheme, 76 projects worth more than ₹5,295.24 crore were sanctioned across 15 thematic circuits , and 75 projects were physically completed.The circuits include Buddhist, Coastal, Desert, Eco, Himalayan, North-East, Ramayana, Rural, Spiritual and Tribal tourism.

### Swadesh Darshan 2.0
- Swadesh Darshan 2.0 follows a destination-centric approach based on sustainable tourism, experience-based tourism, integrated planning, infrastructure and improved visitor experiences.
- Under the scheme, 53 projects worth ₹2,207.08 crore have been sanctioned.

### PRASHAD
- PRASHAD stands for Pilgrimage Rejuvenation and Spiritual, Heritage Augmentation Drive .
- Launched in 2014-15, it provides financial assistance to States and Union Territories for developing pilgrimage and heritage destinations.
- Under PRASHAD, 54 projects worth ₹1,726.18 crore have been sanctioned.
- The scheme supports tourist facilitation centres, sound-and-light shows, parking facilities, ghats, solid-waste management systems, solar panels and CCTV installations.

## Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment
- The SASCI scheme supports iconic tourist destinations through improved infrastructure, connectivity, visitor facilities and local employment.

- Under the amended 2025-26 guidelines, eligible States that had not received funds under Part III of SASCI 2024-25 could receive up to ₹250 crore under Part II.

## Why in News?
The union home minister has proposed linking major rivers from the Brahmaputra to the Godavari and Cauvery to address long-term water scarcity. However, unresolved inter-State disputes, ecological risks, displacement and limited progress in existing projects have renewed debate over the feasibility of large-scale river interlinking

## What is Interlinking of Rivers?
- The Interlinking of Rivers (ILR) programme is a large-scale water-management initiative that seeks to transfer water from water-surplus basins to water-deficit regions.
- The transfer is proposed through a network of reservoirs, dams, canals and diversion structures.
- The programme is based on the assumption that some river basins have surplus water that can be transferred to regions experiencing shortages.

### Inter-basin Water Transfer
- Inter-basin water transfer means moving water across the natural boundary of one river basin into another.
- Supporters view it as a way to redistribute water, while critics question whether a river basin can be permanently classified as surplus because water availability changes across seasons and years.

## Historical Background
- The idea of linking Indian rivers dates back to the 19th century, when Sir Arthur Cotton proposed river-based irrigation and navigation projects.
- The proposal gained attention after Independence due to India’s uneven rainfall distribution and recurring floods and droughts.
- In 1980, the Ministry of Water Resources formulated the National Perspective Plan for interlinking rivers.
- The National Water Development Agency (NWDA) was established in 1982 to examine the feasibility of river-linking projects.

## National Perspective Plan
- The National Perspective Plan identified 30 major river links .
- These are divided into two components: 14 links under the Himalayan component and 16 links under the Peninsular component .

### Himalayan Component
- It broadly seeks to link rivers originating in the Himalayan region and transfer water across northern and eastern river basins.

### Peninsular Component
- It focuses on linking rivers in peninsular India to transfer water between relatively water-rich and water-deficit regions.

### Role of the NWDA
- The NWDA studies the feasibility of river-linking proposals.
- Its work includes assessing river flows, identifying possible links and examining whether water transfers can be technically implemented.
- The agency maintains that river-link planning accounts for the need to improve or protect river flows during lean periods.

## Objectives of River Interlinking

### Water Security
- The programme seeks to transfer water from relatively water-rich basins to water-deficit areas and reduce regional disparities.

### Irrigation Expansion
- Additional water could expand irrigation, support crop diversification, increase agricultural productivity and improve farmers’ incomes.

### Flood and Drought Management
- Supporters argue that excess water during floods could be diverted to water-scarce regions, reducing the impact of floods and droughts.

### Drinking and Industrial Water
- Inter-basin transfers could improve water availability for cities, industries and rural communities facing shortages.

### Hydropower Generation
- Reservoirs and related infrastructure may provide opportunities for generating hydropower.

### Balanced Regional Development
- Improved irrigation, water supply, navigation and rural infrastructure could support employment and economic development in water-deficit regions.

### Integrated River-basin Management
- River interlinking may encourage coordinated planning of water resources across political and administrative boundaries.

## Inter-State Water Disputes in Southern India
- Several southern rivers flow through more than one State, resulting in disputes over dams, irrigation projects, reservoir operations and water sharing during periods of scarcity.
- The delay in resolving existing disputes demonstrates the difficulty of achieving the level of inter-State cooperation required for large-scale river interlinking.

### Pennaiyar River Dispute
- Tamil Nadu requested the Union government in November 2019 to constitute a tribunal for adjudicating its Pennaiyar river dispute with Karnataka.
- Tamil Nadu alleged that Karnataka had violated the 1892 inter-State agreement and also approached the Supreme Court.
- Two negotiation committees were constituted and 11 meetings were held, but the dispute remained unresolved.
- The Supreme Court directed the Centre to constitute a tribunal and later extended the deadline by six months. However, the tribunal had not been established at the time covered by the source.

### Proposal to Use the Mahadayi Tribunal
- The Union government asked whether the Pennaiyar dispute could be referred to the Mahadayi Water Dispute Tribunal instead of constituting a separate tribunal.
- The Pennaiyar and Mahadayi disputes concern different river systems and geographical regions.
- The article argues that such a transfer is not permitted under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 .

### Mekedatu Project Dispute
- Karnataka’s proposed Mekedatu dam project has faced opposition from Tamil Nadu over concerns about downstream water availability.
- Tamil Nadu requested the Union government in March 2026 to constitute a tribunal on the project.
- The Centre had not responded to this demand at the time covered by the source.

## Arguments in Favour of River Interlinking

### Improving Agricultural Productivity
- Additional irrigation could increase agricultural output, support crop diversification and improve farmers’ incomes.
- The Indira Gandhi Canal Project helped expand irrigation and agriculture in arid parts of Rajasthan.

### Enhancing Water Security
- Water could be transferred from relatively water-rich basins to drought-prone and water-deficit regions.
- The Ken–Betwa Link Project, for example, aims to provide irrigation and drinking water to the Bundelkhand region of Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh.

### Managing Floods and Droughts
- Diversion of excess water during high-flow periods could reduce flood pressure while supporting water-scarce regions.
- Proponents argue that this could improve India’s long-term water resilience.

### Supporting Urban and Industrial Needs
- Inter-basin transfers can help meet the drinking-water requirements of expanding cities.
- The Krishna Water Supply Project transfers Krishna river water to meet Hyderabad’s drinking-water needs.

### Reducing Regional Imbalances
- Transferring water to dry regions could promote settlement, agriculture and economic development in areas with limited natural water availability.

## Arguments Against River Interlinking

### Difficulty in Identifying Surplus Water
- A river may carry excess water during a good monsoon but experience scarcity during a weak rainfall year.
- Calculations based on average flows may not accurately reflect seasonal variations, drought years or future climate conditions.

### Climate-change Uncertainty
- Climate change is altering rainfall patterns, glacier behaviour and river flows.
- These changes make assumptions about permanent water-surplus and water-deficit basins increasingly uncertain.

### Ecological Effects
- River ecosystems are interconnected. Changing natural flows can affect aquatic biodiversity, fish migration, wetlands, sediment transport, groundwater recharge and downstream ecosystems.
- Diversions may also alter the ecological balance of donor and recipient river basins.

### Impact on the Vembanad Wetland
- Kerala opposes the Pamba–Achankovil–Vaippar Link Project .
- It argues that diverting water from the Pamba and Achankovil rivers could harm the Vembanad wetland ecosystem , into which these rivers drain.

### Claims During Distress Years
- Recipient regions may begin to treat transferred water as a permanent entitlement.
- They may continue demanding water during drought years, potentially reducing supplies available to the original basin.

### Inter-State Water Conflicts
- Rivers are shared by multiple States, making allocation politically sensitive.
- The Pennaiyar and Mekedatu disputes demonstrate the difficulty of securing agreement on river-water use.

### Land Acquisition and Displacement
- Large dams, canals and reservoirs require extensive land.
- Such projects may displace local and tribal communities and affect livelihoods, cultural connections and access to natural resources.

### Economic and Technical Difficulties
- River-linking projects involve high financial costs, complex engineering, continuous maintenance and long gestation periods.
- Growing resistance to land acquisition can further delay implementation.

### Diversion from Sustainable Alternatives
- Excessive dependence on mega engineering projects may reduce attention to water conservation, watershed development, groundwater management and efficient use.

## Successful Inter-basin Transfer Projects

### Mullaperiyar Dam Project
- The project involves Kerala and Tamil Nadu.
- Water from the Periyar basin in Kerala is diverted to the Vaigai basin in Tamil Nadu for irrigation and drinking-water purposes.
- It has supported agriculture in the drought-prone regions of Tamil Nadu.

### Parambikulam–Aliyar Project
- The project involves Kerala and Tamil Nadu.
- It is a multi-reservoir inter-State project that diverts waters from rivers of the Western Ghats to provide irrigation benefits in Tamil Nadu.
- It represents cooperation between States in sharing river water.

### Krishna Water Supply Project
- The project is associated with Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh.
- It transfers Krishna river water to meet the drinking-water requirements of Hyderabad.
- It demonstrates the use of inter-basin transfers to address urban water shortages.

### Indira Gandhi Canal Project
- The project involves Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan.
- It transfers water from the Sutlej–Beas river system to the arid regions of Rajasthan.
- It transformed parts of the Thar Desert by expanding irrigation, agriculture and settlement.

### Limited Number of Projects
- India has implemented only a handful of major inter-basin water-transfer projects during the last 130 years.
- Their limited number demonstrates the technical, financial, political and social difficulties involved.

## Status of River-linking Efforts
- After returning to power in 2014, the Union government constituted a Special Committee for Interlinking of Rivers .
- The committee has held more than two dozen meetings.
- Despite these meetings, the national river-linking programme has made limited progress.
- Major barriers include State consent, water-sharing arrangements, environmental concerns, land acquisition, rehabilitation and questions over surplus-water availability.

## Ken–Betwa Link Project
- The project seeks to transfer water from the Ken basin to the Betwa basin.
- It aims to provide irrigation and drinking-water benefits to drought-prone Bundelkhand in Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh .
- In 2024, the Prime Minister laid the foundation stone for the ₹44,000-crore project .
- The project has faced protests from tribal communities in Chhatarpur over displacement, environmental effects and loss of traditional livelihoods.
- It shows that technical and financial approval alone cannot ensure ecological sustainability or social acceptance.

## Supply-side and Demand-side Approaches

### Supply-side Approach
- A supply-side approach tries to increase water availability through dams, reservoirs, canals and inter-basin transfers.
- River interlinking follows this approach because it transports additional water to areas experiencing shortages.

### Demand-side Approach
- Demand-side management focuses on conserving existing water, reducing wastage and improving water-use efficiency.
- It includes micro-irrigation, groundwater conservation, rainwater harvesting, watershed management and restoration of traditional water bodies.
- It also involves sensitising and incentivising farmers to use irrigation water efficiently.
- The article argues that governments and civil society should prioritise demand management instead of continuously depending on mega supply-side projects.

## Challenges
Federal and Governance Challenges
- States may disagree over surplus-water calculations, project designs, costs and allocation arrangements.
- Delays in creating tribunals and implementing decisions weaken dispute-resolution mechanisms.
Ecological Challenges
- Altered river flows may affect biodiversity, wetlands, sediment movement, groundwater recharge, fisheries and downstream ecosystems.
- Climate change makes long-term water-availability estimates increasingly uncertain.
Social Challenges
- Large projects can displace local and tribal communities.
- Fair compensation, rehabilitation and livelihood restoration remain difficult.
Economic and Technical Challenges
- Mega projects involve high financial costs, complex engineering, long construction periods and continuous maintenance.
- Extensive land acquisition can delay projects and increase opposition.
Water-use Challenges
- Additional water may not solve scarcity if agricultural consumption remains inefficient.
- Indiscriminate groundwater extraction, including extraction encouraged by free agricultural electricity, can worsen ecological stress.
Institutional Challenges
- More than two dozen committee meetings have not produced substantial progress.
- Limited consensus among States can delay individual links and the wider programme.

## Way Forward
Strengthen Water-governance Institutions
- The National River Linking Project and ILR programme should follow a scientific, transparent and participatory approach.
- The NWDA should conduct detailed basin-level studies before recommending transfers.
Improve Dispute Resolution
- Mechanisms under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 should be strengthened to ensure timely tribunal formation and implementation of decisions.
- The constitutional framework under Article 262 should support equitable and cooperative management of inter-State rivers.
Adopt Basin-level Planning
- Planning should consider the entire river basin, including downstream users, wetlands, ecological flows and seasonal variations.
- States, technical experts, local communities and other stakeholders should participate in decision-making.
Introduce Climate-risk Assessment
- Project planning should account for changing rainfall patterns, glacier behaviour, droughts and extreme weather.
- Surplus-water calculations should not depend only on past average flows.
Strengthen Environmental Safeguards
- Projects should undergo credible environmental-impact assessments.
- Ecological flows, aquatic biodiversity, sediment movement, groundwater recharge and downstream ecosystems must be protected.
Ensure Rehabilitation and Community Participation
- Affected communities should participate in project planning, compensation, rehabilitation and livelihood restoration.
- Tribal and local rights must be considered before land acquisition.
Prioritise Demand-side Management
- India should expand micro-irrigation, groundwater conservation, rainwater harvesting, watershed management and restoration of traditional water bodies.
- Farmers should be sensitised and incentivised to use water efficiently.
Regulate Groundwater Extraction
- Indiscriminate extraction should be curbed, particularly where free electricity encourages excessive pumping.
Examine Local Alternatives First
- Local water harvesting, aquifer recharge, efficient irrigation and restoration of tanks should be examined before approving mega transfers.
Promote Cooperative Federalism
- The Centre and States should rely on transparent data, consultation and mutually accepted water-sharing arrangements.
- Integrated river-basin management can reduce conflicts and improve long-term water security.

## Conclusion
River interlinking may support water security in carefully selected cases, but it cannot be a universal solution. India needs a balanced strategy based on conservation, efficient use, groundwater management, ecological protection and cooperative federalism.
