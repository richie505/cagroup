India–Denmark Partnership to Strengthen Amaravati’s Power Infrastructure

### Relevance: GS Paper II – International Cooperation | GS Paper III – Energy Infrastructure, Renewable Energy and Urban Development
For Prelims: INDEP | 33-kV Power Grid | Distributed Energy Resources | BESS | Digital Twin | APCRDA For Mains: Smart Grid | Energy Transition | Urban Resilience | Renewable-Energy Integration | International Technical Cooperation

## Why in News?
A Danish delegation visited Amaravati under the India–Denmark Energy Partnership (INDEP) and held discussions with the Andhra Pradesh Capital Region Development Authority (APCRDA) on strengthening the capital city’s power infrastructure.
The discussions focused on designing a 33-kV power grid capable of meeting Amaravati’s future electricity demand.

## Objectives of the Visit
The meeting at the APCRDA headquarters in Rayapudi examined:
- planning and design of the proposed 33-kV power network;
- expansion of grid capacity;
- integration of renewable energy;
- use of energy-storage systems;
- development of electric-vehicle charging infrastructure;
- adaptation of Danish power-sector practices; and
- further technical cooperation.

## Technologies Discussed

### 1. Distributed Energy Resources
- Distributed Energy Resources (DERs) are small-scale electricity sources or storage systems located close to consumers.
- They may include rooftop solar systems, local battery units and other decentralised energy facilities. Their integration can reduce pressure on the main grid and improve local energy resilience.

### 2. Battery Energy Storage Systems
A Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) stores electricity for later use.
It can:
- store surplus renewable power;
- supply electricity during peak demand;
- manage fluctuations in solar and wind generation; and
- improve grid reliability.

### 3. Electric-Vehicle Charging Infrastructure
- Planning charging stations along with the power grid can ensure adequate electricity supply for the future growth of electric mobility.

### 4. Renewable-Energy Integration
- Renewable sources require flexible grid management because their generation may vary with weather conditions. Storage, forecasting and grid-control systems can help manage these variations.

## Role of the Digital Twin
- APCRDA presented Amaravati’s development plans through a digital twin at the Amaravati Integrated Command Control Centre.
- A digital twin is a virtual representation of a physical system that can be used to visualise, monitor and plan infrastructure.
It displayed:
- ongoing works;
- proposed projects;
- urban infrastructure; and
- the planned power network.
This can help planners examine future demand, compare alternatives and identify infrastructure gaps before physical construction.

### Participants
The Danish delegation included representatives from:
- Danish Energy Agency;
- Green Power Denmark; and
- Central Electricity Authority.
APCRDA officials from administration, economic development, information technology and electrical engineering participated in the discussions.

## Conclusion
India–Denmark cooperation can help Amaravati develop a reliable, efficient and clean power network. However, technology transfer must be supported by careful planning, cybersecurity, skilled personnel and long-term maintenance.

One Nation, One Time: Making IST Legally Enforceable

### Relevance: GS Paper II – Governance | GS Paper III – Science and Technology, Digital Infrastructure and Cybersecurity
For Prelims: IST | UTC+5:30 | CSIR-NPL | Legal Metrology | Atomic Clock | NTP | PTP | NavIC | RRSL | White Rabbit Technology For Mains: Time Sovereignty | Digital Evidence | Critical Infrastructure | Network Synchronisation | Cybersecurity | Technological Self-Reliance

## Why in News?
The Union government notified the Legal Metrology (Indian Standard Time) Rules, 2026 on August 27, 2026.The Rules make Indian Standard Time (IST) the single reference for legal, administrative, commercial and other official purposes across India. They will come into force 180 days after publication in the Official Gazette , allowing organisations to modify their systems.

## What Is Indian Standard Time?
Indian Standard Time is India’s official time reference.
- International reference: Coordinated Universal Time
- Offset: UTC+5 hours 30 minutes
- Maintained by: CSIR–National Physical Laboratory
- Generated using: Advanced atomic clocks
- Trademark: IST® registered by CSIR-NPL in 2024
CSIR-NPL is India’s National Metrology Institute , responsible for maintaining the country’s scientific measurement standards.

## What Has Changed?
- India has followed IST for decades. However, time itself previously lacked enforceable legal status. Organisations could use different time sources without proving that their clocks were connected to IST.
- Under the new Rules, organisations must ensure that their systems are synchronised with time traceable to IST .
- The change is comparable to the regulation of other measurements. Legal Metrology authorities already verify weights and volumes; they can now enforce a common legal standard for time.

## Why Is a Common Time Reference Needed?
Modern digital systems depend on accurate timestamps. If different organisations use different time sources, their clocks may vary by milliseconds, seconds or even minutes.
This can affect:
- banking and digital payments;
- stock exchanges;
- telecommunications and internet services;
- railways, airports and transport systems;
- national power grids;
- government and legal records;
- digital evidence and cyber investigations;
- navigation and scientific research; and
- emergency and time-critical services.
A common reference ensures that events and transactions occurring across different systems can be accurately compared and reconstructed.

## What Does “Traceable to IST” Mean?
- Traceability means that a system’s clock has an unbroken and verifiable connection to the national time standard maintained by CSIR-NPL.
- For example, a computer becomes traceable to IST when it synchronises with an approved Network Time Protocol server connected to the national reference.
Traceability can be established through:
- Network Time Protocol;
- Precision Time Protocol;
- satellite links;
- NavIC;
- optical-fibre links; or
- other approved Indian timing sources.
The appropriate method depends on the level of accuracy required.

## How Is IST Generated and Distributed?

### 1. Generation by CSIR-NPL
CSIR-NPL generates IST using advanced atomic clocks. It maintains India’s time within a few nanoseconds of UTC, with an uncertainty of less than three nanoseconds .

### 2. Regional Distribution
Five Regional Reference Standard Laboratories (RRSLs) maintain secondary time scales traceable to CSIR-NPL. This enables IST to be distributed from multiple locations.

### 3. Network Synchronisation
CSIR-NPL, the National Informatics Centre and RRSLs provide time services. Organisations can connect their systems to approved servers.

### 4. Satellite Dissemination
ISRO maintains traceability to IST through satellite links. NavIC satellites and ground systems help distribute accurate Indian timing signals.

## Time-Synchronisation Technologies
| Technology | Typical accuracy | Main use |
| NTP | Milliseconds | Banking, offices and ordinary networks |
| PTP | Microseconds | Telecom, industrial and local networks |
| Satellite links | Nanoseconds | Space, defence and navigation |
| Optical links | Picoseconds in specialised systems | Advanced research laboratories |

### Network Time Protocol
- NTP is the most accessible method and works through the Internet. It regularly compares a device’s clock with an authorised server and corrects any difference.
- Periodic correction is necessary because every clock or oscillator gradually drifts after synchronisation.

### Precision Time Protocol
- PTP provides greater accuracy than NTP. It is especially effective within local area networks and is suitable for industrial and telecom applications.

### Satellite and Optical Links
- Satellite methods, including Common-View GNSS and two-way satellite time transfer, provide nanosecond-level precision. Dedicated optical-fibre links can reach picosecond-level accuracy but are not widely available.

## Does Every User Need Nanosecond Accuracy?
No. Accuracy depends on the application.
- Ordinary computer networks may use millisecond-level NTP.
- Industrial and telecom networks may require microsecond-level PTP.
- Banking and financial services may use accurate NTP-based systems.
- ISRO, defence and scientific institutions may require nanosecond precision.
The Rules create one national reference but do not require every organisation to install an atomic clock.

## White Rabbit Technology
- In July 2026, a White Rabbit Technology-based IST Dissemination Demonstration Network was commissioned at the Regional Reference Standard Laboratory in Bengaluru.
- White Rabbit combines precise time synchronisation with network communication. The pilot demonstrated the distribution of high-accuracy IST for:
- high-frequency financial markets;
- stock exchanges;
- digital banking;
- telecommunications;
- national power grids; and
- other critical systems.

## Compliance and Enforcement

### Requirements for Organisations
An organisation must be able to demonstrate that:
- its systems are synchronised with IST;
- it receives time from an approved source;
- its timestamps meet the required accuracy; and
- traceability can be verified through technical records.
Computer settings, selected time servers, PTP connections or satellite links may be used as evidence of compliance.

### Display of Other Time Zones
There is no general exemption at present. Hotels, international organisations or space centres may display other time zones when required, provided that they also display IST and use it as the principal legal reference.

### Enforcement Authority
The Legal Metrology Department will enforce compliance. CSIR-NPL will provide technical guidance because it maintains the national time standard and helped establish the dissemination system.

### Impact on Ordinary Users
- Ordinary users are unlikely to face any immediate compliance requirement. Service providers will make most technical changes.
- Once service providers comply, mobile phones, computers and online services should automatically receive time traceable to IST.
- Accurate time can help courts and investigators reconstruct events relating to digital fraud, cybercrime and disputed electronic transactions.

## Why Reduce Dependence on GPS?
Many Indian networks currently receive time directly or indirectly from the United States-owned Global Positioning System .
A foreign system may technically be degraded, manipulated or denied during a crisis. India’s experience during the Kargil War, when access to requested GPS information was denied, strengthened the demand for an independent navigation system.
ISRO subsequently developed the Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System , now called NavIC.
Role of NavIC
- Provides regional navigation coverage over India and surrounding areas.
- Carries atomic clocks aboard its satellites.
- Maintains ground systems traceable to IST through CSIR-NPL.
- Distributes secure and reliable Indian timing signals.
- Reduces dependence on foreign navigation and timing systems.
CSIR-NPL is also developing indigenous primary frequency standards and advanced atomic clocks to ensure continuity during external disruption.

## Significance
Digital and Financial Integrity
A common timestamp establishes the correct sequence of payments, trades and banking transactions.
Cybersecurity
Synchronised logs help identify cyberattacks, trace malicious activities and reconstruct digital incidents.
Critical-Infrastructure Resilience
Telecom networks, transport systems and power grids require precise time coordination for safe operation.
Legal Certainty
IST-traceable timestamps strengthen electronic records, contracts, government files and digital evidence.
National Security
Indian timing sources reduce exposure to interruption or manipulation of foreign satellite signals.
Technological Self-Reliance
CSIR-NPL’s atomic clocks, RRSLs and NavIC strengthen India’s sovereign digital infrastructure.

## Challenges
- Cost of upgrading older systems
- Limited technical capacity among small organisations
- Different accuracy needs across sectors
- Inadequate connectivity in remote areas
- Cyberattacks on national timing infrastructure
- Clock drift and equipment failure
- Need for clear inspection and certification procedures
- Dependence on imported precision components

## Way Forward
- Issue sector-specific accuracy and compliance standards.
- Expand timing infrastructure through the five RRSLs.
- Establish geographically separated backup time centres.
- Combine NTP, PTP, satellite and optical channels.
- Conduct regular cybersecurity and resilience audits.
- Promote indigenous atomic clocks and timing equipment.
- Train Legal Metrology officers and technical personnel.
- Provide compliance support to smaller organisations.
- Maintain verifiable logs of continuous synchronisation.
- Periodically test NavIC and emergency backup systems.

## Conclusion
The IST Rules transform Indian Standard Time from a widely followed convention into an enforceable national standard. A secure timing network built around CSIR-NPL, RRSLs and NavIC can strengthen India’s digital economy, critical infrastructure and technological sovereignty.

Q1. Consider the following statements regarding the Legal Metrology (Indian Standard Time) Rules, 2026:
- They make IST the legal reference for official and commercial purposes.
- They require every organisation to maintain its own atomic clock.
- They provide a 180-day transition period.
Which of the statements given above are correct?
(a) 1 only (b) 1 and 3 only (c) 2 and 3 only (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (b) 1 and 3 only
Explanation
- Statement 1 is correct: The Rules make IST compulsory for legal, administrative, commercial and other official purposes.
- Statement 2 is incorrect: Organisations may synchronise through approved NTP, PTP, satellite or other timing services. They need not maintain their own atomic clocks.
- Statement 3 is correct: A transition period of 180 days has been provided for necessary system changes.
Q2. Consider the following statements regarding time-synchronisation technologies:
- NTP generally provides millisecond-level accuracy.
- PTP can provide microsecond-level accuracy within local networks.
- NavIC is entirely dependent on GPS for its timing reference.

(a) 1 only (b) 1 and 2 only (c) 2 and 3 only (d) 1, 2 and 3
Answer: (b) 1 and 2 only
Explanation
- Statement 1 is correct: NTP commonly provides millisecond-level synchronisation through network servers.
- Statement 2 is correct: PTP offers microsecond-level accuracy and works effectively within local area networks.
- Statement 3 is incorrect: NavIC is India’s independent navigation system, with timing traceable to IST through CSIR-NPL and ISRO’s ground infrastructure.

## FAQs
1. Why were new IST Rules required?
IST was already followed, but its adoption was not legally enforceable across official and commercial systems.
2. What does “traceable to IST” mean?
It means that a system’s time has a verifiable connection to the national reference maintained by CSIR-NPL.
3. Must every business install an atomic clock?
No. Most businesses can synchronise through approved NTP servers or other authorised services.
4. Can other time zones be displayed?
Yes. However, IST must also be displayed and used as the primary legal reference.
5. Will ordinary users need to change their devices?
Generally, no. Compliant service providers should automatically supply IST-traceable time.
6. Why is NavIC important?
NavIC provides an indigenous navigation and timing channel, reducing dependence on foreign systems such as GPS.

26th SCO Summit: India Calls for Unified Action Against Terrorism

## Why in News?
Prime Minister Narendra Modi participated in the 26th Meeting of the SCO Council of Heads of State , held in Bishkek, Kyrgyz Republic, from August 31 to September 1, 2026.The summit marked the SCO’s 25th anniversary . India called upon members to dismantle the complete ecosystem of terrorism, including financing, recruitment, radicalisation and safe havens.Pakistan assumed the SCO chairmanship for 2026–27 .

## About the SCO
The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) is a Eurasian grouping working on security, economic cooperation, connectivity and regional stability.
Origin and Expansion
- 1996: Shanghai Five formed by China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan.
- 2001: SCO established in Shanghai with Uzbekistan.
- 2017: India and Pakistan became full members.
- 2023: Iran joined.
- 2024: Belarus became the tenth member.
Member States
India | China | Russia | Pakistan | Kazakhstan | Kyrgyzstan | Tajikistan | Uzbekistan | Iran | Belarus
Permanent Bodies
- SCO Secretariat: Beijing, China
- Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure: Tashkent, Uzbekistan
Shanghai Spirit
The “Shanghai Spirit” emphasises mutual trust, sovereign equality, non-interference and mutually beneficial cooperation. SCO decisions are generally taken through consensus.

## India’s Three-Pillar Vision
India’s approach to the SCO rests on:
1. Security
Combating terrorism, extremism, radicalisation, drug trafficking and other transnational threats.
2. Connectivity
Developing trusted transport and trade links that respect the sovereignty and territorial integrity of every country.
3. Opportunity
Expanding cooperation in technology, innovation, entrepreneurship, start-ups, youth engagement and people-to-people exchanges.

## India’s Call Against Terrorism
India called for committed action against the entire terrorist ecosystem rather than relying only on an “action-reaction” approach.
The Prime Minister identified four connected elements:
- terror financing;
- recruitment;
- radicalisation; and
- safe havens.
India argued that terrorism must never be treated as a strategic asset or instrument of state policy. It also rejected double standards in which some terrorist groups are condemned while others receive protection or support.
The Bishkek Declaration similarly described attempts to use terrorist, separatist or extremist groups for political gain as unacceptable.

## Afghanistan and Drug Trafficking
India linked Eurasian security with peace and stability in Afghanistan.
It highlighted:
- India’s development and humanitarian assistance;
- the need for lasting peace and security;
- the regional threat posed by drug trafficking; and
- the importance of preventing Afghan territory from contributing to instability.
The Bishkek Declaration called for an inclusive government in Afghanistan with the participation of all ethnopolitical groups.

## West Asia and Iran
India noted that conflict in West Asia affects:
- energy security;
- maritime security;
- global supply chains; and
- the economies of the Global South.
The Bishkek Declaration expressed deep concern over developments in the Persian Gulf region, supported Iran’s sovereignty and called for a diplomatic resolution.
It also criticised unilateral deployment of global missile-defence systems when such actions negatively affect international security.

## Peaceful Use of Nuclear Energy
SCO members affirmed the inalienable right of countries to:
- develop and use atomic energy for peaceful purposes; and
- pursue equal, sustainable and mutually beneficial international nuclear cooperation without discrimination.
The summit also adopted decisions relating to nuclear safety.

## Connectivity and Economic Cooperation
India supported connectivity initiatives that link markets and expand trade but maintained that they must respect sovereignty and territorial integrity.
The wider SCO agenda included:
- transport corridors;
- trade facilitation;
- energy cooperation;
- investment;
- digital technology;
- environmental protection; and
- sustainable development.

## Proposed SCO Development Bank
SCO members welcomed ongoing work towards establishing an SCO Development Bank and agreed to complete consultations for a founding document.
The proposed institution could finance:
- infrastructure;
- transport networks;
- investment projects; and
- regional development initiatives.
Its success will depend on agreement over capital contribution, governance, lending priorities and institutional control.

## Major Summit Outcomes
The summit adopted:
- the Bishkek Declaration ;
- four Memoranda of Understanding; and
- more than 20 decisions and statements.
These covered:
- counterterrorism;
- climate change;
- nuclear safety;
- energy conservation;
- drug-addiction control;
- economic cooperation;
- youth engagement; and
- cultural exchange.
Pakistan assumed the next SCO chairmanship, while Lahore was designated the SCO Tourism and Cultural Capital for 2026–27 . Kyrgyzstan also named one of its mountain peaks the “SCO Peak.”

## India’s Initiatives Recognised

### English in the SCO Charter
India’s proposal to include English as an official language of the SCO found a place in the summit outcomes. Traditionally, Russian and Chinese have been the organisation’s official languages.

### Civilisation Dialogue Forum
India offered to host the first SCO Civilisation Dialogue Forum later in 2026 to promote exchanges among member states’ ancient civilisations.
Young Scientist Forum
The forum seeks to promote collaboration among young researchers and encourage scientific innovation.

### Young Authors’ Conclave
The conclave promotes literary and cultural engagement among young writers from SCO countries.

## Significance for India
- Provides sustained engagement with Central Asia.
- Supports cooperation against terrorism and drug trafficking.
- Enables dialogue with major Eurasian powers.
- Provides a forum to discuss Afghanistan.
- Creates opportunities in trade, energy and technology.
- Allows India to advocate sovereignty-based connectivity.
- Strengthens India’s role in a multipolar international order.
- Expands civilisational and youth exchanges.

## Challenges
- India–Pakistan differences over cross-border terrorism
- India–China disputes over territory and connectivity
- Divergent positions on Eurasian and West Asian conflicts
- Limited direct Indian access to Central Asia
- Consensus-based decision-making can delay action
- Possibility of declarations remaining unimplemented
- Competition over the proposed Development Bank’s governance
- Risk of geopolitical tensions weakening security cooperation

## Way Forward
- Strengthen RATS and financial-intelligence cooperation.
- Develop common standards against terror financing and safe havens.
- Ensure that connectivity projects respect sovereignty.
- Convert summit declarations into time-bound programmes.
- Advance consultations on the SCO Development Bank transparently.
- Expand cooperation in healthcare, climate action and digital technology.
- Improve India–Central Asia transport and trade connectivity.
- Promote an inclusive and stable Afghanistan.
- Use scientific, youth and cultural forums to build long-term trust.

## Conclusion
The Bishkek Summit expanded the SCO’s agenda from regional security to connectivity, development and culture. However, its credibility will depend on consistent action against terrorism, respect for sovereignty and implementation of agreed projects.
