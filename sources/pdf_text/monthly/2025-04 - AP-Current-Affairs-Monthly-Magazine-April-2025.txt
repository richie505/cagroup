Why in News

• India reported its second human death due to H5N1 (bird flu) in a two-year-old girl from Palnadu, Andhra Pradesh, highlighting concerns over zoonotic disease transmission and food safety.

Introduction

• The confirmation of H5N1 infection in a two- year-old girl from Palnadu district, Andhra

Pradesh, has raised concerns about avian influenza in India.

• The child, who passed away due to multi- organ failure at AIIMS-Mangalagiri on March 16, was diagnosed with H5N1 by the National Institute of Virology (NIV), Pune, on March 31.

• This marks the second human fatality due to the virus in India and the first in Andhra Pradesh.

H5N1 Virus: An Overview

• H5N1 is a highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) virus primarily affecting birds. While rare, human infections can occur through direct contact with infected poultry, contaminated surfaces, or consumption of undercooked poultry products.

• The virus has a high fatality rate among infected humans, making it a significant public health concern.

Case Details: H5N1 Infection in Palnadu

• The two-year-old girl developed symptoms including fever, cough, diarrhoea, seizures, and breathlessness on February 28.

• She was admitted to AIIMS-Mangalagiri on March 4, where her condition worsened despite treatment. The child was also diagnosed with leptospirosis, which likely contributed to multi-organ failure.

• Interestingly, Palnadu district did not report any prior bird flu outbreaks, making the source of infection uncertain.

• However, officials revealed that the child had a habit of consuming raw chicken, which might have exposed her to the virus.

Response Measures

Following the confirmation of H5N1, health authorities initiated containment measures:

• Fever Surveillance: Rapid Response Teams (RRTs) from Guntur General Hospital and eight health teams conducted surveys in the affected area. No abnormal cases were detected, but monitoring will continue for two weeks.

• Testing of Close Contacts: Family members, including parents, grandparents, and a four- year-old sibling, tested negative. Their nasal swabs are under analysis.

• Public Advisory: Authorities emphasized the importance of cooking meat and eggs properly to prevent infections.

Human Risk and Preparedness

While H5N1 transmission from birds to humans is rare, its high mortality rate necessitates vigilance. Human-to-human transmission remains unconfirmed, but continuous surveillance is crucial. Key preventive measures include:

• Avoiding contact with sick or dead poultry.

• Ensuring thorough cooking of meat and eggs.

• Strengthening veterinary monitoring to detect bird flu outbreaks early.

• Enhancing public awareness about safe food practices.

• Later, in the 1960s, a more successful iteration was seen in Mizoram, where strategic resettlement culminated in a peace accord. Yet, the application of this strategy to the tribal context of central India has resulted in more harm than resolution.

The Gutti Koya Dilemma

Most of the displaced tribals from Chhattisgarh belong to the Gutti Koya community, who have settled in forest lands of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. While a few have joined security forces or integrated locally, the majority continue to face systemic marginalisation:

• Dispossession of land and housing: Farmlands cultivated by tribals have been seized for afforestation; newly built homes are routinely destroyed by forest officials.

• Legal invisibility: Despite being Scheduled Tribes (STs) in Chhattisgarh, they are not granted tribal status in Andhra Pradesh or Telangana, rendering them ineligible for tribal welfare schemes.

• Fear of Maoists: Many tribals are unable to

return due to threats from Maoists, who view them as collaborators with the State.

• Hostility from local communities: Local tribals and forest officials often view them as encroachers, leading to conflict and harassment.

Forest Rights Act (FRA), 2006 – A Missed Opportunity

• Clause 3.1(m) of the FRA states that tribals who vacated forest land before the cut-off date of 13 December 2005 are entitled to alternative forest land. Many displaced tribals have applied under this clause in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.

• However, the Chhattisgarh government has failed to process or support these claims, citing administrative and jurisdictional reasons, even though inter-state land allocation is not prohibited by law.

No National Framework for IDPs

• India lacks a dedicated national policy or legal framework for Internally Displaced Persons. Unlike the Bru Rehabilitation Plan of 2019 — which allowed Bru tribals to settle in Tripura with central assistance — Gutti Koyas have received no such support.

Right to Education (RTE) Act, 2009

• The Right to Education (RTE) Act, passed in 2009, is a landmark piece of legislation in India that guarantees free and compulsory education for all children between the ages of 6 and 14.

• This act was implemented to fulfill the constitutional mandate of Article 21-A, which provides the fundamental right to education to children.

Key Features of the RTE Act

1\. Compulsory Education for Children Aged 6-14 Years The RTE Act makes it mandatory for children between the ages of 6 and 14 to receive free and compulsory education in a school. It places a legal obligation on both the state and private schools to admit children and provide education without charging any fees. 2. Free Education The act ensures that children receive education without the financial burden of tuition fees. This includes all expenses related to schooling, such as uniforms, books, and transportation, in government schools. 3. Provision of Quality Education The RTE Act emphasizes quality education, setting guidelines for student-teacher ratios, infrastructure, and facilities required in schools. This ensures that children are not only enrolled but also provided with an environment conducive to learning. 4. No Detention Policy Under the RTE Act, children are not to be held back or detained in the same grade until the completion of the elementary stage (Class 8). This aims to reduce drop-out rates and promote inclusive learning. 5. Reservation for Disadvantaged Groups Section 12(1)(C) of the RTE Act mandates that 25% of seats in private unaided schools be reserved for children from economically weaker sections (EWS) and disadvantaged groups. This initiative ensures equitable access to quality education for all.

6\. Child-Friendly Environment The act advocates for child-friendly practices in schools, which include the removal of corporal punishment, and focuses on creating a non-threatening, supportive environment for children. 7. Accountability and Monitoring The RTE Act provides for the establishment of school management committees to ensure the smooth implementation of its provisions. It also mandates the formation of regulatory bodies at the national, state, and district levels to monitor the quality of education. 8. No Capitation Fees: The RTE Act prohibits schools from charging capitation fees or making admissions subject to donations, which ensures that education remains affordable and accessible to all. 9. Infrastructure Standards The act sets clear guidelines on the minimum infrastructure standards required for schools, including the number of classrooms, play areas, and sanitation facilities.

• Indicated: 2,369 MT

• Inferred: 778 MT In addition, the State holds 7.69 million tonnes of crude oil (1.15% of national reserves) and 59.27 billion cubic metres (BCM) of natural gas (5.42%), primarily from offshore sources.

• While these resources strengthen energy security, they also pose challenges to India's climate commitments unless a balanced energy mix is ensured.

Challenges Hindering Renewable Energy Expansion

1\. Land Acquisition Bottlenecks Acquiring land for solar parks and wind farms, especially in ecologically sensitive and densely populated regions, has delayed multiple projects. Coastal regulation zones and forest clearance norms add layers of complexity. 2. Financial Constraints Renewable energy projects demand substantial upfront capital. Many developers struggle with high interest rates and limited access to long-term credit, which affects project viability. 3. Grid Infrastructure and Integration Issues The lack of sufficient evacuation infrastructure and storage capacity leads to curtailment of renewable power, especially during peak generation hours. Grid stability remains a concern. 4. Biomass Resource Limitations The growth of biomass and cogeneration is constrained

by irregular supply chains, competing agricultural needs, and the seasonal availability of feedstock.

Opportunities and Recommendations

Experts have highlighted a number of pathways for Andhra Pradesh to tap into its renewable potential:

• Large-Scale Wind and Solar Projects AP should prioritise investment in utility- scale renewable energy parks, particularly in wind-rich coastal districts and solar- intensive inland areas.

• Streamlining Approval Mechanisms A single-window clearance system, along with rationalisation of land use norms, can ease project delays and attract private investment.

• Storage and Grid Modernisation Investing in battery storage, smart grids, and flexible transmission infrastructure will help balance the variability of renewable sources and improve reliability.

• Public–Private Partnerships (PPPs) Encouraging PPPs in both generation and distribution can enhance efficiency, reduce costs, and spur innovation.

• Balancing Energy Mix While leveraging existing coal reserves, AP must ensure a gradual transition to clean energy. Integrating rural solar initiatives, such as solar irrigation pumps and rooftop solar for households, can foster inclusive energy growth.

Conclusion

• With over 94% of its renewable energy potential yet to be utilised, Andhra Pradesh stands at a critical juncture. Strategic policy interventions, financial incentives, and infrastructure development can transform the State into a renewable energy leader.

• In doing so, AP can contribute significantly to India’s Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) under the Paris Agreement and support its vision of a low- carbon, sustainable future.

Key Highlights of the Discovery

• Date and Location: o The copper plates are dated Saka 1341, Sarvari, Magha Sudha Triodasi, which corresponds to January 28, 1420 CE. o Found in Srisailam Devasthanam, one of the twelve Jyotirlingas of Lord Shiva, in present-day Andhra Pradesh.

• Language and Format: o The inscription is engraved on three

copper leaves. o It is bilingual, written in both Sanskrit

and Telugu.

Historical Significance

• Ruler Involved: o The inscription attributes the restoration to Recharla Lingama Nayaka, a ruler from the Recharla dynasty, who reclaimed territories earlier occupied by the Turakas (a reference to Muslim invaders or rulers).

• Territorial Restoration: o Recharla Lingama Nayaka restored eight Agraharas (tax-free land grants to Brahmins and temples) and associated vṛittis (revenue-yielding lands) in the following regions:

▪ Munaluri-Seema: Vodanekalapalli, Mukundavaram, Singapuram, Basavapuram

▪ Gudpalli-Seema: Pudinadulu

▪ Charikonda-Seema: Autupalli

▪ Devarakonda-Seema: Juvunemutlu,

Suddapalli

▪ Other villages: Regadevulapalli, Vuggulapalli, Ganapuram

Restoration of Temple Services

Due to Muslim occupation, various religious and charitable services at the Srisailam temple were interrupted. These services were reinstated after the region was reclaimed:

• Nityaapahara – Daily food offerings to the deity.

• Akhanda-Dipala-Chamuru – Supply of ghee for perpetual lamps.

• Nivalipalyalu-Nityasatram – Running daily almshouses.

• Sivaratri-Mahotsavam – Celebration of Maha Shivaratri festival.

• Bhuri Satram – Pilgrim accommodations in choultries.

Epigraphic Importance

• Dr. K. Munirathnam Reddy, Director (Epigraphy), ASI Mysore division, emphasized the first-time usage of the term ‘Swarajya Punarudhharana’ in a Telugu inscription.

• The term signifies the reclamation of religious and political sovereignty over a prominent cultural-religious centre following foreign occupation.

Link to Freedom Movement

• Bala Gangadhara Tilak, a key nationalist leader, popularized the phrase ‘Swarajyam Naa Janma Hakku’ (Swarajya is my birthright).

• Tanguturi Prakasam Pantulu later adopted the concept and established the newspaper ‘Swarajya’ in three languages, emphasizing national self-rule.
