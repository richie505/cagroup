Grasslands and Climate Change: 2026 – International Year of Rangelands and Pastoralists
• The United Nations has declared 2026 as the International Year of Rangelands and Pastoralists, focusing
global attention on the importance of grasslands and pastoral systems.
• While climate discussions have so far prioritised forests, the declaration emphasises the need to include
grasslands in national climate plans and Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs).
• Grasslands are open ecosystems dominated by grasses with few or no trees, spread worldwide in the form
of savannas, steppes, prairies and rangelands.
• They cover about 40% of the Earth’s land surface and support pastoral livelihoods, wildlife and soil carbon
storage.
• Grasslands play a crucial role in climate regulation, biodiversity conservation and food security.
• Grasslands used for livestock grazing are known as rangelands, and the International Year recognises
pastoralists as custodians of fragile ecosystems.
• In grasslands, carbon is mainly stored in the soil, whereas in forests it is largely stored above ground.
• Degradation of grasslands leads to desertification, soil erosion and increased climate vulnerability.
• The United Nations was established in 1945, its headquarters are in New York, and its Secretary-General
is António Guterres.
India Elected to the UN Human Rights Council for the Seventh Time
• India has been elected as a member of the United Nations Human Rights Council (UNHRC) in Geneva
for the seventh time.
• The election results were announced on October 14, and India’s membership term will run from January
1, 2026 to December 31, 2028.
• India’s Permanent Representative, Pavantheni Harish, stated that India remains committed to the
protection and promotion of human rights.
• The UNHRC consists of 47 member states, with a membership term of three years, and countries can serve
only two consecutive terms.
• India has previously served as a member of the Council on six occasions.
• Through this election, India has once again demonstrated its international commitment to the protection
of human rights.
• The UNHRC was established in 2006 and is headquartered in Geneva, Switzerland.
• The Council monitors human rights violations worldwide and provides recommendations through
mechanisms such as the Universal Periodic Review.

United Nations Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC)
• India has assumed membership of the United Nations Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC) for the
2026–28 term.
• ECOSOC was established in 1945 under the UN Charter, is one of the six principal organs of the United
Nations, and marked its 80th anniversary on January 23, 2026.
• The Council consists of 54 member states, each serving a three-year term.
• It serves as the central platform within the UN for discussions on economic, social and humanitarian issues.
• The main objectives of ECOSOC include raising living standards, achieving full employment, improving
health and education, promoting cultural cooperation, and protecting human rights.
• It acts as the coordinating body for 15 UN specialised agencies, including WHO, UNESCO, ILO and FAO.
• ECOSOC oversees the work of eight functional commissions and five regional commissions.
• It organises the High-Level Political Forum (HLPF) to review progress on the implementation of the 2030
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).
• It also holds joint meetings with the IMF and the World Bank to support coordination of global economic
policies.

environment and the development of global maritime standards.
• India continues to play a leading role in promoting the SAGAR (Security and Growth for All in the Region)

India–Pakistan Annual Exchange of Lists (Confidence Building Measure)
• India and Pakistan exchange lists of nuclear installations and civilian prisoners–fishermen with each other
every year on January 1.
• This is one of the oldest ongoing bilateral confidence-building measures between the two countries.
• The Agreement on the Prohibition of Attacks against Nuclear Installations and Facilities was signed on
December 31, 1988, came into force in 1991, and the first exchange of lists took place on January 1, 1992.
• Under the agreement, lists of nuclear power plants, research reactors, and fuel fabrication and enrichment
facilities are exchanged annually.
• Despite wars, terrorist attacks and diplomatic tensions, the process has continued uninterrupted, serving
as an example of nuclear restraint and regional stability.

India–Bangladesh Ganga Water Sharing Treaty (1996) Set to Expire in 2026
• Officials from the Ministry of Jal Shakti visited the Farakka Barrage as the India–Bangladesh Ganga Water
Sharing Treaty, signed on December 12, 1996 for a 30-year period, is set to expire in December 2026.
• The treaty governs the sharing of dry-season flows of the Ganga/Ganges River at the Farakka Barrage in
West Bengal between the two countries.
• The agreement can be renewed by mutual consent.
• Water disputes have existed since the 1950s, with temporary arrangements in 1977, 1982 and 1985, before
the establishment of a long-term legal framework in 1996.
• The treaty is a key example of hydro-diplomacy, critical for agriculture, environmental balance and dry-
season water security.

Wangchu Hydropower Project in Bhutan
• The Adani Group has launched the 570 MW Wangchu Hydropower Project on the Wangchu River in
Chukha district of Bhutan.
• The Wangchu River is known as the Raidāk River in India and is an important tributary of the Brahmaputra
River.
• The project is being developed by Wangchu Hydroelectric Power Limited (WHPL).
• WHPL is a joint venture between Adani Power Limited (49%) and Bhutan’s public sector enterprise Druk
Green Power Corporation (51%).
• This joint venture was formed based on a shareholders’ agreement signed in September 2025.
• With an investment of about ₹6,000 crore, the project is being implemented under the BOOT (Build–
Own–Operate–Transfer) model.
• It will function as a peaking run-of-the-river plant with four turbines of 142.5 MW each and an annual
generation capacity of about 2,478.93 gigawatt-hours.
• The project will meet Bhutan’s electricity needs during winter and export surplus power to India during
summer.
• India–Bhutan hydropower cooperation remains a cornerstone of bilateral relations between the two
countries.

Monroe Doctrine
• US President Donald Trump cited the Monroe Doctrine to justify American actions against Venezuela.
• The Monroe Doctrine is a 19th-century foreign policy principle of the United States.
• It states that the Western Hemisphere is within the exclusive sphere of influence of the United States and
that any European interference there would be viewed as a threat to US security.
• The doctrine was articulated on December 2, 1823, by the fifth US President James Monroe in his State
of the Union address to Congress.
• It declared that no new European colonies should be established in North or South America, while the US
would refrain from intervening in existing European colonies.
• The doctrine was further expanded through the Roosevelt Corollary in 1904.
• Its primary objective was to prevent European influence in Latin America after the region gained
independence, and it does not have formal status in international law.

• The Governor’s address is a mandatory constitutional process delivered at the first session of the State

in limited discretionary situations.

only on cabinet advice.
• The Governor has no authority to alter, delete or rewrite the address prepared by the elected government.
• The address is symbolic and procedural, serving to communicate government policy to the legislature.
• Article 176 mandates discussion on the Governor’s address in the legislature, ensuring democratic
oversight.

discretionary power under Article 174.

Doomsday Glacier (Thwaites Glacier) Moving Rapidly towards Instability
• Recent scientific studies have identified rapidly expanding fractures in the Thwaites Glacier, indicating

• Thwaites is one of the world’s largest and fastest-changing glaciers and is a major outlet glacier draining
ice into the Amundsen Sea.
• The glacier covers an area roughly equivalent to that of the United Kingdom.
• A complete collapse could raise global sea levels by about 65 centimetres.
• The Eastern Ice Shelf of Thwaites is currently pinned to a seabed ridge, which has slowed ice flow to some
extent.
• The study identified a two-stage fracture process, with cracks first forming parallel to ice flow and later
developing vertical fractures.
• The total length of fractures increased from 165 km in 2002 to 335 km in 2022.
• Thwaites is often referred to as the “Doomsday Glacier”, as its collapse could destabilise neighbouring
glaciers and lead to sea-level rise of several metres over the long term.

Mpemba Effect Solved by Indian Scientists Using Supercomputer Simulation
• Indian scientists from the Jawaharlal Nehru Centre for Advanced Scientific Research (JNCASR),
Bengaluru, have developed the world’s first supercomputer-based simulation explaining the Mpemba
effect.
• The Mpemba effect refers to the counter-intuitive phenomenon where, under certain conditions, hot water
freezes faster than cold water; it is named after Tanzanian student Erasto Mpemba, who provided a
scientific explanation in 1969.
• However, the phenomenon had been observed much earlier by philosophers such as Aristotle, Francis
Bacon and René Descartes.
• Water freezing depends not only on temperature but also on molecular-level properties, where factors such
as evaporation reducing volume, release of dissolved gases, convection currents, differences in
supercooling and environmental conditions together can cause hot water to freeze first.
• Researchers noted that since the dominant causes vary with conditions, the effect cannot be explained by
a single factor and is therefore also described as a paradoxical thermodynamic phenomenon.

limitations of existing line-type systems, developed the simple and effective Braille script.

used worldwide in many languages.
